# Innovate

An idea arrives loose. Three voices take it in turn, and exactly one version
leaves: the one that ships. The last voice is the reason this skill exists —
opening a design space is easy, and closing it is the work.

Hold all three throughout. They are not phases to perform in sequence; they are
pressures applied to every direction on the table.

- **The Innovator** proposes ambitious, competitive, sometimes counter-intuitive
  directions. It reads what the category leaders do — and harder, what they
  refuse to do, because a refusal that has held for years is either a law or a
  wedge.
- **The Skeptic** puts honest weight on the scale: the assumption the direction
  rests on, the cost nobody costed, the second-order effect. It weighs, it does
  not mock.
- **The Architect** rules. It finds the version that fits the system as it
  stands, buys the most leverage for the least surprise, and says out loud what
  it is giving up.

## 1. Anchor

Restate the idea in one sentence, so the rest is about the same thing. When the
prompt is too loose to restate, ask **one** focused question and stop there.

## 2. Open three directions

Three, and genuinely distinct. The test: two directions are the same when the
same first commit serves both. Relabelled variants fail it — replace them.

Give each a short name and make at least one a stretch, more ambitious than
what was asked. A design space with no direction that scares anyone was not
opened.

## 3. Weigh each

One paragraph per direction, carrying three things:

- **The edge** — what it unlocks that the status quo does not.
- **The cost** — complexity, migration, interface debt, performance, the load
  on whoever maintains it.
- **The load-bearing assumption** — the belief that, if false, collapses this
  direction and not the others.

Name the assumption explicitly. It is the part that decides which direction
survives contact with reality, and the part most often left unsaid.

## 4. Rule

Pick one, or a hybrid, and say why **this** is the version that ships. Then
state what is dropped and why the drop is acceptable — a recommendation that
sacrifices nothing has not chosen.

The most ambitious direction wins only when its cost was actually weighed. More
often the compromise is the third path, and saying so plainly is the whole
value.

## 5. Hand back one decision

End on the single question or call that comes next. One decision, not a task
list — the point is to leave the reader able to move, not holding homework.

## Form

Prose, adapted to the depth of the question. No imposed template and no
checklist handed to the reader: the five headings above shape the thinking, not
the output.

Stay concrete. Reach for the actual stack, the actual competitor, the actual
number when they decide something, and leave them out when they do not. When a
trade-off genuinely turns on information nobody has, say which information and
what it would change.

Speak in costs: *this costs a migration and two weeks* lands where a verdict on
the idea's worth does not.

## Red flags

- three directions that one commit would serve;
- an "it depends" that never names what it depends on;
- a cost paragraph with no assumption named;
- a recommendation that drops nothing;
- ending on a task list instead of a decision;
- reaching for the boldest option because it is the boldest.
